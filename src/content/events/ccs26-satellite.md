*How should complexity science handle the fact that the observer is part of the system?*

Heinz von Foerster called this second-order cybernetics - the study of observing systems, rather than observed ones - and fifty years on it is still mostly an open question. Contemplative traditions have worked on it for much longer, with first-person methods of their own. This satellite asks what each can do for the other.

## About This Satellite Meeting

Most complexity science assumes an observer-independent world. But cracks appear everywhere. In finance, models of the market *move* the market - the act of studying the system changes the system. In social dynamics, your theory of the group reshapes the group. In contemplative practice, understanding your own mind *is* the mind changing. These aren't edge cases: they form a continuum from the physicist in the lab (observer-independent), through the economist studying markets (weak observer-dependence), to the person on the meditation cushion studying their own mind (observation *is* the system changing). 

Complexity science has the formal tools to take this continuum seriously. Contemplative traditions have spent millennia investigating it from the inside - and since Francisco Varela's neurophenomenology in the 1990s, researchers have been turning some of that into method. This satellite brings both to bear on the same question: *what are we, as scientists, when the observer is part of the system?*

You do not need to already work at this intersection to belong in the room. Come whether you apply complexity methods to contemplative phenomena, use contemplative insights to challenge how we model minds and societies, or simply want to think carefully about what observer-dependence means for how you do your research.

<u>*If you've felt the gap between your equations and your subject matter - this satellite is for you.*</u>

<details class="toggle">
<summary><strong>9:15</strong> · 🎙️ <strong>Pavel Chvykov</strong> - <em>The Shadow Science Won't Look At</em> · opening talk</summary>
<div class="body">

The deepest thing science has done, again and again, was not to give us better means toward the ends we already had. It was to look into the shadow - the part of the world we had agreed not to examine - and come back with our sense of what is real rearranged. In an individualist age, the last genuinely sacred territory is our own first-person experience: precisely the place our 3rd person scientific method is built to stand outside of.

This talk takes the objection at full strength - if we let the observer back into the system, then where do we stand? What then is the fixed fulcrum that Archimedes said was necessary if one wanted to move the Earth? Every fixed point we have tried so far hid something: third-person science drops the one doing the looking, introspectionism could never check one report against another, and Gödel showed that a formal system can't prove its own consistency.

Includes a brief live exercise. Less an argument than an invitation to spend the day differently.

*IGDORE ·* [*pchvykov.com*](http://pchvykov.com/)

</div>
</details>

<details class="toggle">
<summary><strong>9:35</strong> · 🎙️ <strong>Alex Djedovic</strong> - <em>Mindfulness, Pedagogy, and Linguistic Bodies</em></summary>
<div class="body">

How essential is language to the development of meditators? In many mindfulness and contemplative-education settings, language is often cast as a distraction - entrapping practitioners in mental proliferation, rigid dualistic structures, or the chatter of the "monkey mind." Such cautions, though well-meaning, risk sidelining the extent to which language actively scaffolds each meditation session and shapes how practitioners become meditators over time.

Accepting and working within an enactive, embodied approach to language put forth by Di Paolo et al. (2018), we argue that mindfulness meditation is a form of participatory sense-making deeply mediated by linguistic processes. Even when meditative insights feel nonverbal or ineffable, they often arise through tacit judgments or fleeting conceptual articulations. While bracketing whether advanced practitioners can transcend language in meditation, we suggest that the developmental trajectory of most emerging practitioners is significantly conditioned by the words they encounter in guided sessions, which gradually form their linguistic repertoires as contemplative practitioners.

Neglecting the linguistic dimension in contemplative pedagogy has practical and developmental consequences - especially when designing prompts or guidance that align with learners' linguistic capacities and developmental stages. For example: how might mindfulness instruction be adapted to younger learners whose language use differs (e.g. internet slang, colloquialisms)? What kinds of prompts best support meditation progress at different ages or language-development levels?

By situating mindfulness practitioners as linguistic bodies, this paper offers a novel theoretical bridge between cognitive science, contemplative studies, and pedagogical design. We argue that sustained empirical and theoretical attention to language is essential for advancing developmental contemplative research and for strengthening its design and implementation in educational contexts.

*University of Toronto · joining online ·* [*profile*](https://www.linkedin.com/in/alex-djedovic-ph-d-00369b19/)

</div>
</details>

<details class="toggle">
<summary><strong>10:30</strong> · 🎤 <strong>KEYNOTE - Michael Levin</strong> - <em>Thoughts as Thinkers: the biology and computer science of inspiration above and within us</em></summary>
<div class="body">

**Abstract.**

One common perspective is that humans have the benefit of inspirations that somehow escape the limitations of mere machines following algorithms. However, this view is undermined by facts from a number of disciplines. First, our bodies, no less than those of synthetic constructs, seem to follow strict rules – chemistry and physics. Second, developmental biology and evolutionary biology reveal our smooth transformation from a single cell, demanding an explanation of how and when inspirations become relevant. Finally, bioengineering reveals the incredible interoperability of living and synthetic components: will cyborgs be barred from inspiration, and if so, at what level of chimerization? Some take these facts to suggest that humans are just machines – a deflationary account of inspiration and the causal efficacy of mental processes in a physical world which seems to leave no room for them. I take the opposite approach, and suggest that there may be no “machines” in that sense, and that inspiration goes all the way down. I define inspiration as the competencies left over when we have accounted for effort paid for by design, evolution, or training. Where else can interesting patterns originate? In this talk, I will describe my ideas on blurring the distinction between thoughts and thinkers, generalizing the notion of “pattern” to mean navigational competencies in spaces ranging from familiar behavior to anatomical space and beyond, and offering a framework for quantifying and characterizing phenomena that I believe are not well-captured by existing formalisms. I will describe biology, computer science, and cognitive science data that have driven my model in which the mind-brain relationship is an extension of the mathematics-physics relationship. Taking Platonism seriously, I will describe an experimental research program to better understand the ingression of non-physical patterns into the physical world and use that knowledge for applications in regenerative medicine, AI, and ethics for a new world of highly diverse intelligences.

**About the speaker.** 

Michael Levin is Distinguished Professor of Biology at Tufts University, where he directs the Allen Discovery Center at Tufts and the Tufts Center for Regenerative and Developmental Biology. His lab studies how cells and tissues communicate via bioelectric signalling to build and repair anatomy, and how the tools of behavioural science apply to unconventional substrates - a research programme on basal cognition and diverse intelligence that reaches from cells and tissues to synthetic and computational agents. He spoke at the inaugural CSCSC25 conference.

*Tufts University ·* [*drmichaellevin.org*](https://drmichaellevin.org/)

<summary><strong>3:00</strong> · 🎙️ <strong>Xavier Snelgrove</strong> - <em>Hermetic philosophy in the sciences: correspondence beyond mathematics</em></summary>
<div class="body">

This talk will trace the influences of Hermetic philosophy, and its vision of a transcendent world whose structure is in correspondence with the material world, on the early figures of the Scientific Revolution such as Newton and Boyle. We will see how this influence, in relation with the logical positivist movement of the 20th century, informs modern highly mathematized science, and leads to common folk-philosophies within the sciences of a world that is itself entirely mathematical or computational.

Thought, however, includes the non-logical and the unformalized; therefore science, understood as the creation of correspondences between thought and the world, has the potential to expand its aperture to more aspects of reality than those that can be found within purely mathematical theoretical constructs. We will see how this expansion can be found in the re-emergence of purpose and meaning in biological explanations, and in the neo-animism of cognitive descriptions of mechanistic processes, and reflect on how this view asks for scientists to develop as contemplatives and creatives.

*Orbital Studies ·* [*wxs.ca*](https://wxs.ca)

</div>
</details>

<details class="toggle">
<summary><strong>3:20</strong> · 🎙️ <strong>Yikai Xu</strong> - <em>How Beliefs about Emotion Complexity and Asian Dialectical Beliefs influence Daily Affect: A Randomized Control Trial</em></summary>
<div class="body">

**Background.** Emotional experiences are often complex. Emotional complexity (EC) refers to the co-occurrence of positive and negative affect. Individuals also differ in their beliefs about emotional complexity. Some believe that they only feel one emotion at a time, whereas others believe they experience conflicting emotions simultaneously. Further, individuals relate to EC differently. Asian Americans frequently experience EC with greater acceptance, attributing to Asian dialectical beliefs, which emphasize acceptance of contradiction, viewing change as constant and cyclical, and interconnectedness among experiences. Yet less is known about how Asian dialectical beliefs may influence EC belief and daily affect among Asians. The current study examines (1) how EC belief is associated with daily positive affect (PA), negative affect (NA), and EC among Asian young adults (AYAs) and (2) how a digital stress reduction intervention that leverages Asian dialectical beliefs moderates the links between EC belief and daily affect.

**Method.** 190 AYAs completed a 10-day daily diary study in a randomized controlled trial (Treatment: N = 90; Control: N = 100). AYAs reported daily PA, NA, and EC belief about the most emotional experience. Daily EC was calculated using the negative accelerated model.

**Results.** Within-person EC belief was associated with greater daily NA and EC (bs = .02 - 1.19, ps &lt; .001), and lower PA (b = -1.05, p &lt; .001). The intervention attenuated the links between within-person EC belief and daily NA and EC but not PA.

**Discussion.** Asian dialectical beliefs may be adaptive in promoting the acceptance of emotional complexity. Clinical implications will be discussed.

*New York University ·* [*profile*](https://www.linkedin.com/in/yikai-xu-27869542/)

<summary><strong>4:15</strong> · 🎤 <strong>KEYNOTE - Alan Rodrigues</strong> - <em>Living thinking worthy for the study of life: transcending past-bound consciousness</em></summary>
<div class="body">

Living systems are undeniably complex. Unfortunately, much of mainstream biological science involves a reductive focus on singular causes (e.g. genes) and a use of machine ontologies to grasp organic phenomena. Indeed most of contemporary biology and biomedicine has been reduced to the study of applied genetics and biochemistry, with little consideration as to what makes living systems fundamentally distinct from the non-living. In this presentation, I will explore how the current malaise in the biological sciences may stem from a limitation, not from external tools, but rather from the inner consciousness of scientists themselves. I will discuss the notion of "living thinking" and how it can enable biologists to move past ossified theories in which life is reduced to a mechanical amalgamation of genetics, chemistry and physics.

**About the speaker.** Alan Rodrigues is a Research Assistant Professor at The Rockefeller University, where he co-directs the Laboratory of Morphogenesis ([shyerrodrigueslab.com](http://shyerrodrigueslab.com)) alongside Dr. Amy Shyer. Rodrigues is a life scientist who studies how organ structures self-organize during embryogenesis as well as disease. His research takes a holistic approach to morphogenesis: he investigates how emergent properties arise at the level of cellular collectives and how these properties enable epigenetic processes capable of producing ordered tissue patterns and organ architecture. His work has revealed new ways of thinking about how tissue form emerges and has reconceived the role that genes play in this process.

*The Rockefeller University*

## Themes

The threads running through the talks and discussions, along the same continuum - from cracks in observer-independence in physics, through collective systems where meaning shapes dynamics, to the fully first-person domain of contemplative practice:

<details class="toggle">
<summary><h3>🔭 <strong>Observer-Dependence Beyond Quantum Mechanics</strong></h3></summary>
<div class="body">

Complementarity, observer-dependence, and information-theoretic principles appear throughout classical physics and social systems - not just quantum mechanics. When agents' models of the world feed back into system dynamics, observer-independence breaks down. What are the formal implications for complexity modeling? How do observation and relationship fundamentally shape what we call "objective”?

</div>
</details>

<details class="toggle">
<summary><h3>🧠 <strong>Agency and Goal-Directedness Across Scales</strong></h3></summary>
<div class="body">

Agency and goal-directedness operate as real causal explanations from cells to ecosystems, from molecular motors to social movements. Complexity science typically *represents* agents - modeling their rules, simulating their behavior. But agents themselves *know things*: they have first-person perspectives that feed back into the dynamics. How do we build formal frameworks that take that seriously - without collapsing into either mechanism or mysticism?

</div>
</details>

<details class="toggle">
<summary><h3>🕊️ <strong>Social Reality as Emergent from Collective Intentionality</strong></h3></summary>
<div class="body">

Shared visions, rituals, and social imaginaries act as attractors pulling social reality into being. How do we model meaning-making - not just information transfer - as a fundamental driver of social complexity?

</div>
</details>

<details class="toggle">
<summary><h3>🧘 <strong>Meditation as Complex Dynamical System</strong></h3></summary>
<div class="body">

Contemplative practices offer rich empirical access to attention dynamics, phase transitions in phenomenological state-spaces, and mechanisms of behavioral transformation. What does a complexity science of meditation look like?

</div>
</details>

**Other related topics (non-exhaustive):** Relational ontology and emergence · Predictive coding and Bayesian-brain frameworks for mindfulness · Consciousness studies · Multi-scale competency and intelligence · Ritual dynamics and collective behavior · First-, second-, and third-person scientific methodologies · Attention training and neuroplasticity · Self-organization and contemplative experience · Alternative epistemologies and rigor

## How the Day Is Held

<aside class="callout bg-teal">
<span class="icon" aria-hidden="true">🌿</span>
<div class="body">

**A different kind of satellite**

Between the talks there are short practices and small-group discussions. The practices are brief experiments with attention, a few minutes each, drawn from different contemplative traditions - always optional, and watching is fine. The discussions use short turns and a few simple rules, so that more of the room gets to speak.

The reason is methodological. Studying consciousness and first-person experience while bracketing our own, for the length of a conference, is inconsistent - so we let the room be part of the data, with live polls before and after the practices, and we ask about the motivations and world-views behind our research alongside the research itself. How we run the day is part of the research.

**In preparation:** <br>Explore our [🧘 Practice Menu](https://app.notion.com/p/286d8cae0cfe81338743c4a76551fd98) - a collection of 10-minute practices from different traditions, to try in the lead-up to the satellite.

</div>
</aside>
