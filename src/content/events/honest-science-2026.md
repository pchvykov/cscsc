## The core idea

What actually drives your science? Not the fundable version—the real thing. The questions you think about in the shower. The worldview you're not sure you're allowed to admit. The intuitions that feel too personal to put in a paper.

> Most of us have forgotten, or learned not to say. We hide behind methodology. We perform "serious scientist" because the alternative feels too vulnerable, too crackpot, too intimate.

The reason this is so hard isn't just social pressure. Most of science trains you to avoid these questions entirely. In simple systems, you don't need to ask them—reductionism gives you a default worldview for free. But the more you work with complex systems, the more that default starts to strain. Emergence, agency, observer-dependence, meaning—these aren't questions you can keep deferring. They shape what you study, how you model it, and what you count as an explanation. At some point, you have to face them.

<aside class="callout">
<span class="icon" aria-hidden="true">✨</span>
<div class="body">

**This workshop is about facing them together.**

The honest science we seek already exists—it's happening in the margins, in the conversations we're "not allowed" to have, in the questions that drive us that don't fit in grant proposals. This workshop brings that science from the margins to the center, for one week, to see what becomes possible.

</div>
</aside>

## What we'll actually do

The workshop runs two intertwined arcs across the week. They support each other: the inner work makes the research more honest; the research gives the inner work substance and direction.

<div class="columns" style="--cols:50.0fr 50.0fr">
<div class="col">

<aside class="callout bg-blue">
<span class="icon" aria-hidden="true">🔬</span>
<div class="body">

**The research arc**

Every participant presents their work—not the polished conference version, but the real questions underneath. We map the field's landscape together through structured discussions (World Café, fishbowl conversations), then self-organize into collaboration sessions around whatever has the most energy. By the end of the week, we're working toward shared research outputs: a collectively authored field-mapping document, collaboration commitments, and communication plans.

</div>
</aside>

</div>
<div class="col">

<aside class="callout bg-purple">
<span class="icon" aria-hidden="true">🪷</span>
<div class="body">

**The inner arc**

You arrive in performance mode. Everyone does. The first days soften that—through nature, honest framing, and listening with curiosity rather than critique. Mid-week, we turn the lens inward: where have incentive structures distorted your scientific judgment? Where are you optimizing for proxies—publications, citations, fundability—instead of the questions you actually care about? This is **epistemic debugging**: noticing where fear, ego, and Goodhart effects have shaped your work. By the end, you articulate what you actually believe and what you're going to do about it.

</div>
</aside>

</div>
</div>

*This is a chance to reflect on our scientific path together – how did we get here? What happened along the way? And where do we actually want to go?*

## Our worldview: the implicit assumptions

Your worldview is the set of deep assumptions that shape your life and research before you even start. Everyone has one. Many scientists have never examined theirs explicitly — which means these assumptions operate invisibly, creating blind spots, unquestioned defaults, and failure modes we can't see because we’re inside them.

Here's what it might look like in practice:

<aside class="callout bg-blue">
<span class="icon" aria-hidden="true">🎯</span>
<div class="body">

**Is science uncovering truth, or creating beautiful frameworks?** If you believe there's an objective reality your models approximate, you optimize for predictive accuracy and treat anomalies as puzzles to solve within the existing paradigm. If you believe science constructs elegant frameworks that organize experience, you optimize for coherence and explanatory power—and you're more willing to abandon a framework entirely when a better one appears. Same data, same equations, but these two starting points lead to genuinely different research programs.

</div>
</aside>

<aside class="callout bg-yellow">
<span class="icon" aria-hidden="true">🐜</span>
<div class="body">

**Do ant colonies "decide" things?** If you think agency requires a brain, you model ant behavior as mechanical response to chemical gradients. If you think agency can emerge in distributed systems, you model the colony as a cognitive architecture. Same ants, same data—each worldview generates its own questions, methods, and explanations.

</div>
</aside>

<aside class="callout bg-teal">
<span class="icon" aria-hidden="true">🧘</span>
<div class="body">

**Is your experience of "insight" during meditation scientifically meaningful?** If first-person experience counts as data, contemplative practice becomes an empirical method and you design studies around it. If only third-person observation counts, you study the same phenomena exclusively through brain scans and behavioral measures. Same phenomenon—two entirely different research programs, each with its own strengths and blind spots.

</div>
</aside>

These aren't idle philosophy. Your implicit answers determine what questions you ask, what evidence you trust, what models you build, and which colleagues you take seriously. And when those answers stay implicit, they become invisible constraints—you rule out entire research directions without realizing you've made a choice.

**This workshop is about making those answers explicit—and discovering what becomes possible when you do.**

<details class="toggle">
<summary><h2>Why complexity science?</h2></summary>
<div class="body">

In simple systems, you rarely need to think about worldview. The reductionist toolkit handles most of what you encounter, and materialism works well enough as a background assumption.

Complex systems put more pressure on those assumptions. When you study emergence, self-organization, collective intelligence, or consciousness, you run into questions that the standard toolkit doesn't fully resolve. What counts as a "cause" when causation flows in multiple directions? What counts as an "agent" when agency is distributed? What's the relationship between the model and the modeler when you're a complex system studying complex systems?

Reasonable people disagree on these questions—and that's precisely the point. They aren't failures of rigor; they're places where worldview choices become scientifically consequential.

**Contemplative practice** enters here as a first-person empirical method—a way to investigate the phenomena (attention, awareness, meaning, emergence-as-experienced) that third-person models point to but can't fully capture. This workshop integrates three epistemic modes:

<details class="toggle">
<summary><h2>The format</h2></summary>
<div class="body">

- 👥 **10–20 participants** (small enough for real intimacy, large enough for intellectual diversity)
- 🌅 **Morning contemplative practice** (optional—yoga, meditation, walking in the Pyrenees)
- ⚡ **Lightning talks** from every participant—the real version, not the conference version
- 🔬 **Structured research discussions**: World Café, fishbowl conversations, collaborative mapping
- 🌀 **Self-organized collaboration sessions** (Open Space format)
- 📝 **Work toward shared research outputs**
- 🍽️ **Long lunches and evenings** for the conversations that actually matter
- 🧘 **Mid-week contemplative day** for integration and epistemic debugging

<aside class="callout">
<span class="icon" aria-hidden="true">🪑</span>
<div class="body">

We're explicitly designing against the stuffy conference vibe. **Circle seating. Questions over statements. Curiosity, not criticism.** But also: real research substance. You leave with collaborations, a publication plan, and clarity on your work.

</div>
</aside>
