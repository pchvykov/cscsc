*Key terms glossary spanning our core areas - complexity science, contemplative studies, philosophy, and cognitive science*

This can also be a quick guide to see what areas you can learn to join the conversation!

## Philosophy

- **Reason** (S. T. Coleridge): The one power that generates all reality, both in nature and in thought, by separating and detaching in order to reunite.
- **Understanding** (S. T. Coleridge): The power of generalizing from data and associating stable names to sense impressions.
- **Imagination** (S. T. Coleridge): The power that crystallizes the ideas of reason into categories of understanding.
- **Idea** (S. T. Coleridge): A constituent "part" of reason, expressed as law in nature and as concept in the understanding.
- **Spirituality**: awareness of the ineffable content of experience and practices for its cultivation
- **Material ontology (ontological realism):** A view that reality is fundamentally made of discrete, independent *things* or substances (e.g., atoms, objects) with inherent properties.
- **Process ontology:** A view that reality is fundamentally made of unfolding *processes*, events, and becoming rather than static things. Being is a verb.
- **Relational ontology:** A view that reality is fundamentally made of the *relationships* and interactions between things. Entities are defined by their connections.
- **Consensus reality:** The agreed-upon view of reality that is constructed and maintained through a shared social and cultural consensus.
- **Post-structuralism:** A philosophical movement skeptical of stable meanings, objective truth, and fixed structures, emphasizing instead how knowledge is shaped by language, power, and context.
- **Being:** The state or essence of existing; the fundamental nature of reality and existence itself.
- **Subject:** The conscious self or mind that perceives, experiences, and acts upon the world.
- **Object:** A thing or phenomenon that is perceived, experienced, or acted upon by a subject.
- **Form:** The essential structure, pattern, or organizing principle of a thing that makes it what it is.
- **Nature:** 1. The inherent character or essence of something. 2. The physical universe and its phenomena as a whole, operating according to discoverable laws.
- **Polarity:** The presence of two opposite, contrasting, yet complementary and interdependent principles (e.g., subject/object, order/disorder, yin/yang).
- **Eudaimonia:** (Aristotle) Often translated as "human flourishing." The state of living in accordance with one's true nature and potential, achieving a life of meaning and virtue. A goal shared by contemplative and complexity-informed practices.

## Contemplative Studies

- **Contemplation:** A practice of sustained, focused attention and open awareness, often aimed at deepening insight into the nature of reality, self, or the divine.
- **Mindfulness:** The practice of paying deliberate, non-judgmental attention to present-moment experience.
- **Ritual:** A formalized, patterned sequence of symbolic activities, often repeated, that connects individuals to community, tradition, and meaning.
- **Ceremony:** A more formal, structured event or occasion that incorporates rituals to mark a significant transition, celebration, or milestone
- **Ecology of practices:** a framework for understanding how a collection of interconnected and interdependent practices work together to foster personal and collective growth, wisdom, and meaning
- **Prayer:** A contemplative practice of communion or communication, often addressed to a divine or transcendent reality, involving gratitude, supplication, or silent presence.
- **Yoga:** (Lit. "union") A diverse set of physical, mental, and spiritual disciplines originating in India aimed at stilling the mind and realizing a unified state of being.
- **Conscious communication practices:** Dialogical practices (e.g., Nonviolent Communication, circling) that emphasize deep listening, presence, and authentic expression to foster mutual understanding.
- **God:** The ultimate, transcendent reality; the ground of all being; a supreme object of worship and devotion. Definitions vary immensely across traditions.
- **Logos:** (Lit. "word," "reason," "principle") A concept representing the underlying ordering principle of the cosmos, divine reason, or the structuring intelligence behind reality.
- **Spirit:** The non-material animating principle of life, consciousness, or divine presence; often contrasted with matter or form.
- **‘gods’ / deities:** Ritually animated, symbolic attractors within a consensus reality that coordinate shared meaning, values, and social cohesion.
- **Interbeing:** (Thich Nhat Hanh) A Buddhist-inspired term expressing profound interdependence and relationality: "to be is to inter-be." A perfect contemplative correlate to relational ontology and networks.

### Religion

- **Religion:** A system of beliefs, practices, rituals, and ethics centered on a relationship with the sacred, transcendent, or ultimate reality, often organized within a community.
- **Faith:** Trust, confidence, or allegiance in something not immediately susceptible to rigorous proof (e.g., a doctrine, a person, a transcendent reality).
- **Tradition:** The transmission of customs, beliefs, and practices from generation to generation, providing continuity and a shared framework of meaning.
- **Dogma:** Static, authoritative constructs that anchor a community's shared beliefs and identity, enforcing doctrinal cohesion.

## Complexity Science

- **Complexity:** The study of systems characterized by a large number of interacting components whose collective behavior is *emergent* and cannot be easily predicted from the properties of the individual parts.
- **Science:** A systematic enterprise that builds and organizes knowledge in the form of testable explanations and predictions about the universe.
- **Law:** A scientific principle that describes a consistent, observable pattern in nature under specific conditions (e.g., law of gravity).
- **Disorder:** A measure of randomness or unpredictability in a system; often quantified as ***entropy***.
- **Emergence:** The process where complex patterns and behaviors *arise* from the interactions of many simple components, without any central control. The whole is greater than the sum of its parts.
- **Self-organization:** The spontaneous emergence of order and structure in a system from local interactions between its components, without external direction.
- **Non-linearity:** A relationship where cause and effect are not proportional. Small changes can have large effects (butterfly effect), and large changes can have small effects.
- **Feedback loops:** A process where the output of a system is circled back as input, influencing subsequent outputs. *Positive* feedback amplifies change; *negative* feedback dampens it and promotes stability.
- **Edge of chaos / criticality:** A transition zone between order and disorder where a system is maximally adaptable, complex, and capable of computation and emergence.
- **Self-organized criticality:** A property of systems that naturally evolve toward a critical state (the edge of chaos) where small perturbations can trigger events of any size (e.g., avalanches in a sandpile).
- **Phase transition:** A radical, qualitative change in the state or structure of a system when a control parameter crosses a critical threshold (e.g., water freezing or boiling).
- **Universality:** The observation that vastly different complex systems (e.g., magnets, forests, financial markets) can exhibit identical critical behavior and scaling laws, suggesting deep common principles.
- **Scale-free behavior:** A pattern where the statistical distribution of events (e.g., size of avalanches, city populations, node connections in a network) follows a power law, meaning there is no characteristic "typical" scale.
- **Causal chains:** A linear sequence of events where one cause leads to an effect, which becomes the cause of another effect.
- **Counter-factual causality:** A form of causation defined not by a direct chain of events, but by the *difference* that an intervention makes. "If X had not happened, Y would not have occurred."
- **Effects of first-order, second-order, higher-order:** The direct and indirect consequences of an action. A *first-order* effect is the immediate result. A *second-order* effect is the consequence of the consequence, and so on.
- **Goodhart’s law:** "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." An indicator loses its value when it is used for control.
- **Braess’s paradox:** The counter-intuitive phenomenon where adding extra resources or pathways to a network (e.g., a new road) can *reduce* the overall system's performance (e.g., increase traffic jams).
- **Typicality:** A property where a single, random instance of a complex system is highly representative of the entire class of such systems, allowing a random model to accurately predict the behavior of a specific, real-world case.
- **Autopoiesis:** (Lit. "self-making") The property of a system that continuously regenerates itself and defines its own boundary. A key bridge between biology (Maturana & Varela) and systems thinking.
- **Resilience:** The capacity of a system to absorb disturbance and reorganize while undergoing change so as to retain essentially the same function, structure, and identity.
- **Sympoiesis:** (Lit. "making-with") A concept contrasting autopoiesis; it describes collectively-producing, boundary-less systems (e.g., ecosystems, coral reefs) that do not have self-defined boundaries. Highly relevant for relational ontology.
- **Semantic Information:** A measure of the *causal, functional, or strategic value* of information or a state for a system in maintaining its existence (e.g., predicting its environment, making a decision).
- **A-life (Artificial Life):** The interdisciplinary study of life and life-like *processes* (e.g., evolution, adaptation, self-organization) by simulating them in computers, robotics, or biochemistry. Its goal is not just to model life-as-we-know-it but to understand the general principles of *life-as-it-could-be*.
- **Semantic Manifold**: A differentiable geometric point-space in which meaning has structure, distances, and curvature. Provides a mathematical substrate for computing relational ontology.
- **Geodesic (In semantic space):** The shortest or most natural path between bounded concepts, given the local curvature of meaning. Represents optimal inference paths and efficient communication routes.
- **Recursive Coupling (Geometric)**: The self-referential influence between points in semantic space, mediated by a rank-3 tensor. Quantifies activity at one location shaping coherence at another via looped feedback. Mathematizes the construction and reconstruction of meaning in complex interdependence.
- **Humility (Geometric)**: A regulatory constraint penalizing recursive complexity beyond context-dependent optimal bounds. Formalizes epistemic restraint as a damping function on excessive self-reference, preventing rigid certainty and adverse amplification.
- **Wisdom (Geometric)**: A regulatory statistical functional integrating coherence, recursive sensitivity, semantic mass, and gradient stability. Forms an emergent field quantifying foresight-driven self-regulation. Distinguished from knowledge by forward-predicting and evaluating anticipated consequences.

### Game Theory

- **Game theory:** The study of mathematical models of strategic interaction among rational decision-makers.
- **Nash equilibrium:** A solution concept where no player can benefit by unilaterally changing their strategy while the other players keep theirs unchanged.
- **Prisoner’s dilemma:** A classic game that shows why two rational individuals might not cooperate, even if it appears to be in their best interest to do so.
- **Pareto optimality:** A state where no individual can be made better off without making at least one other individual worse off.
- **Problem of cooperation:** The challenge of explaining how cooperation evolves in a world of self-interested agents, where defection is often individually beneficial.
- **Problem of coordination:** The challenge of how vast numbers of individuals, without central direction, converge on shared conventions (e.g., language, traffic laws) to create a single, functional societal system.

### Cognitive Science

- **Cognitive science:** The interdisciplinary study of the mind and intelligence, embracing philosophy, psychology, AI, neuroscience, and linguistics.
- **Bayesian inference:** A method of probability-based reasoning where prior beliefs are updated with new evidence to form posterior beliefs.
- **Bayes brain / Predictive processing:** A theory that the brain is a probabilistic inference engine that constantly generates and updates models of the world to predict sensory input.
- **Predictive coding:** A specific framework implementing predictive processing, where the brain minimizes "prediction errors" (the difference between its predictions and sensory data) by either updating its model or acting on the world.
- **Active inference:** The process of minimizing prediction error by selectively sampling the world through action (e.g., moving to get a better view).
- **Free energy principle:** A unifying theory that proposes all self-organizing systems (including brains) act to minimize "free energy," a measure of surprise or prediction error, to maintain their order.
- **Agency:** The capacity of an entity to act intentionally and make choices, influencing its environment and its own states.
- **Enaction:** The view that cognition is not the representation of a pre-given world but is *brought forth* through a history of embodied action within an environment.
- **4E Cognition:** A framework grouping theories of cognition as **E**mbodied, **E**mbedded, **E**nactive, and **E**xtended. Directly links cognitive science to systems thinking.

### Sociology

- **Sociology:** The study of the development, structure, and functioning of human society.
- **Network:** A structure composed of *nodes* (e.g., people, neurons) connected by *links* (e.g., friendships, synapses), defining how information, influence, or resources flow.
- **Opinion dynamics:** The study of how individuals' opinions, beliefs, and attitudes change through social interaction within a network.
- **Memetics:** The theoretical study of ***memes***: units of cultural information (e.g., ideas, styles, practices) that spread from person to person through imitation (like *genes* in biology).
- **Memetic evolution:** The process by which memes vary, are selected, and are transmitted, leading to the evolution of culture, analogous to biological evolution.
- **Social Imaginaries:** The deep-seated, shared understandings and background assumptions that enable common practices and a sense of collective identity in a society.
- **The Social Imaginary (Castoriadis)**: the fundamental capacity for a society to create itself, its own institutions, and its own world.
- **Consensus construct:** A shared belief or social reality that exists because a group of people collectively agree upon it (e.g., money, borders, institutions).
