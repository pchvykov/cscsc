It is worth asking the question honestly, without the usual diplomatic softening on either side. Science has transformed the material conditions of human life at a rate unprecedented in history — food production, disease, life expectancy, the basic infrastructure of comfort and safety. What has it done for the inner life? What has it actually contributed to the project of contemplative development, the transformation of the quality of attention and the sense of self that serious spiritual traditions have been working on for millennia?

The asymmetry is striking when you sit with it. The scientific method has delivered extraordinary results in domains where reality is tractable to third-person measurement — where you can isolate variables, run controlled trials, replicate findings across contexts, and produce knowledge that travels reliably across vast differences of culture and history. But the spiritual path, as most traditions understand it, is aimed at something that resists exactly this structure. It is not primarily about producing propositional outputs or measurable external changes. It is about transforming the quality of the observer — the very thing doing the measuring. And the tools that were designed to study what the observer holds at arm's length are not obviously suited to the project of transforming the observer itself. This is not a contingent limitation waiting to be solved by better methodology. It may be structural, built into what the methods are.

Two concrete proposals tend to come up when this question is pushed seriously: psychedelics and neurofeedback. Both deserve honest evaluation, and both illuminate the possibilities and the limits in specific and instructive ways.

The psychedelic literature is more impressive than its critics acknowledge and more bounded than its enthusiasts tend to admit. Randomised controlled trials combining psilocybin with sustained meditation practice and spiritual support have found measurable, lasting improvements in contemplative depth, psychological wellbeing, and prosocial orientation that exceed what meditation alone produces in equivalent time. A study with experienced meditators found that psilocybin administered during a retreat increased the incidence of self-dissolution experience without accompanying anxiety — something novices typically cannot manage — and produced larger positive changes in functioning at four months compared to placebo. A parallel study using DMT and harmine in a meditation retreat context found similar patterns: deeper mystical experiences and longer-lasting wellbeing improvements than meditation alone. These are real findings, produced by rigorous methods, and they represent a genuine contribution that deserves to be taken seriously.

But the boundary conditions are equally real and worth dwelling on. The contribution consistently requires the contemplative infrastructure the tradition provides. The drug alone does not produce the outcome — the tradition holds, integrates, and makes meaningful what the molecule opens. Remove the practice context, the skilled guidance, the interpretive framework, and the outcomes become less stable, less meaningful, and harder to integrate. There is also a structural problem running beneath this: psychedelics appear to increase the subjective intensity and felt certainty of insights without reliably improving their accuracy. The mechanism that makes an experience feel like genuine breakthrough is precisely the same mechanism that makes false breakthrough feel identical to the real thing from the inside. Science is offering a tool that amplifies both signal and noise proportionally, and that requires the very tradition it is supplementing in order to distinguish between them. This is not a reason to dismiss the contribution, but it is a reason to be precise about what it actually is — and what it depends on.

Neurofeedback sits in a different place but runs into a related wall. The EEG neuroscience of meditation has established reliable correlates of meditative depth: alpha-theta dynamics, gamma coherence characteristic of advanced practitioners, structural and functional brain changes that scale with practice hours. Real-time feedback on these signatures allows practitioners to learn to produce and stabilise relevant states more efficiently than traditional practice alone achieves in equivalent time, particularly in the early stages of concentration development. Several studies show that these changes can persist beyond training sessions into resting states, suggesting the effects are not merely performative.

But the philosophical question is pointed, and it does not dissolve with better data. Is training the brainwave signature the same as developing the underlying capacity? Or is the signature a measurable shadow of something the training is not directly touching — a downstream trace of a process that the feedback loop is approaching from the wrong end? The philosopher Evan Thompson has argued, from within the neurophenomenology tradition, that this is not merely a gap in current knowledge but a structural feature of third-person methods: they can produce correlational accounts of first-person processes but cannot access the processes themselves. The correlation is real. What it is a correlation of remains, in an important sense, inaccessible to the methods being used to measure it. Training the map is not obviously the same as developing the territory, and the difference between the two may be precisely what the traditions are most concerned with.

This points toward the deepest challenge the question opens. If awareness — or whatever language a given tradition uses for its deepest subject — is in some sense primary, and the material substrate is secondary, then using brain imaging to investigate contemplative development may be directed at the wrong level by definition. Not because neuroscience is bad science, but because the assumption about the direction of causality built into the methodology may itself be what is in question. The science cannot adjudicate this without presupposing an answer to it. And this is not a gap that more data will close, because the gap is not empirical — it is about the structure of the inquiry itself.

And yet it would be wrong to end there, because science does offer something genuine even within these limits, and the contribution is worth naming carefully. The epistemic tools — probability, falsifiability, calibrated uncertainty, the systematic practice of looking for disconfirming evidence — are historically new relative to the ancient philosophical and contemplative traditions. They provide something most traditional frameworks do not: a disciplined method for distinguishing genuine signal from noise in claims about the world, for calibrating confidence in reported experiences, for identifying which traditional claims are testable and which are not, and for navigating communities of practice with less dogmatic credulity and more nuanced trust. A practitioner trained in scientific thinking may bring a quality of epistemic care to their practice and their community that is genuinely valuable, and that the traditions themselves did not always cultivate systematically.

The limit is that this contribution operates on the epistemics of the path from the outside, without entering its interior logic. And several traditions suggest that at certain stages, the habits of mind that scientific training most deeply cultivates — independent rational judgment, suspension of belief pending evidence, the maintenance of a critical distance from one's own experience — may become an obstacle rather than an aid. The anti-dogma tools are genuinely useful at the beginning of the path and potentially complicated further along it, which is itself an interesting and underexplored question.

What would a contribution look like that operated at the level of the tradition's own deepest concerns — not measuring the side effects of practice, not perturbing the biological system, but something in the epistemic methods themselves that could be genuinely useful to the interior project the traditions are pursuing? The neurophenomenology programme — treating first-person contemplative report as data on equal footing with third-person measurement, trying to build rigour within the first-person domain rather than translating it into third-person terms — has made the most serious attempt. Whether this constitutes a genuine scientific contribution to the tradition or a sophisticated translation of the tradition into scientific language is a question the literature has not yet resolved.

It may be the most important question the conversation between science and contemplative practice has not yet found a way to ask clearly.
