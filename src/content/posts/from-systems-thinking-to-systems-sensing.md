There is a trajectory running through the history of how we think about complex systems, and it is worth tracing carefully because something interesting happens at its current edge. It begins with the recognition that living systems are not simply complicated machines — that they self-organise, maintain themselves against entropy, and produce structures that could not have been predicted from their parts alone. From Schrödinger's negentropy through Prigogine's dissipative structures to the network science that now maps everything from protein interactions to open-source collaborations, systems thinking has given us increasingly powerful tools for understanding how collective behaviour emerges. The tools are rigorous, externally measurable, and genuinely illuminating.

And yet something keeps escaping them. Network analysis can tell you who is connected to whom, how information flows, where attention concentrates and where it stalls. What it cannot easily tell you is what it feels like to be inside a collaboration that is alive versus one that is going through the motions, or why two teams with identical structural properties can produce radically different work.

This is the opening from which a more recent line of research has been working — moving from systems thinking toward what might be called systems sensing. The distinction is not a rejection of the first tradition but a departure from it. Systems thinking studies social systems from the outside, measuring structure and behaviour with the tools of network science and statistics. Systems sensing attempts something different: to know the system from within, attending to the quality of what is happening in a room, a collaboration, a shared field of attention, before it has been captured in any external measure.

One piece of evidence anchors this shift particularly well. Research on collective intelligence has consistently found that the strongest predictor of a group's performance is not the average skill of its members, nor its structural diversity, but something called social perceptiveness — the capacity to read what is actually happening in another person. This is not a capacity that network diagrams can measure. It is relational, embodied, and developed through a different kind of practice than the ones most research training provides. But the data says it is the primary lever for how well groups think together. Which raises a question the network data alone cannot answer: what are we doing to cultivate it?

The social arts have emerged as one serious attempt at a response. Practices drawn from Social Presencing Theater, Theory U, and what some researchers are beginning to call social somatics are being brought into research contexts not as wellness supplements but as methods — ways of generating knowledge about a collaborative situation that the third-person, externally-measured approach cannot reach. In one study, a daily embodied practice in which two collaborators each moved through a body posture and offered each other a single resonance word produced measurable shifts: bodies moved from closed to open postures within sessions, consistently and significantly, and the language exchanged shifted over time from words expressing uncertainty and instability toward words expressing expansion, flow, and trust. In a longer residency-based programme, statistically significant improvements in emotion regulation and wellbeing were documented, with a replication in a different population showing similar patterns.

The framework holding this work together draws on the idea of a fourth-person perspective: a mode of knowing that is neither the subjective first-person nor the intersubjective second-person nor the objective third-person, but something more like a field-level attunement — sensing from within the collective rather than observing it from without. The practice of listening offers a concrete way into what this means. Listening to a piece of music for facts produces one quality of attention. Listening for emotions produces another. Listening for something like energy or aliveness — a quality that neither the factual nor the emotional register quite captures — produces something else again. The difference between the three is not subtle, and the third is harder to describe afterward without either over-explaining or reaching for metaphor. Which is, perhaps, telling. If three rounds of listening to the same thing can open three genuinely different qualities of attention, then the question of what research might look like if it cultivated the third kind alongside the first becomes less abstract and more urgent.

The tensions in this work are real and worth naming. The evidentiary standards of the social arts are qualitative, experiential, and deliberately pre-propositional — which sits in genuine friction with the p-values and network diagrams that anchor the other end of the same research programme. Most of the applied work is also small-scale, facilitated, and context-specific. How far it travels beyond those conditions is something the research is only beginning to test.

But perhaps the most generative question is also the most basic: if social perceptiveness is the primary lever for collective intelligence, and if the existing architecture of research — its meetings, its formats, its reward structures — is not designed to cultivate it, then something more than a new method is at stake. It is a question about what research institutions are actually for, and what kinds of knowing they are currently leaving on the table.
