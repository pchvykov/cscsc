There is a moment most researchers, artists, and practitioners will recognise. Something catches your attention — a question, a phenomenon, a corner of a field that nobody seems to be paying much attention to — and you feel a pull toward it that is difficult to justify. You cannot yet say why it matters. You cannot point to a gap in the literature and explain how this fills it. You just feel, with some quiet conviction, that there is something here. That this direction opens onto something larger than you can currently see.

The standard institutional response to this feeling is to treat it as preliminary — a starting point that needs to be converted, as quickly as possible, into a properly justifiable research question with precedents, methods, and anticipated outputs. The feeling itself is not the thing. The thing is what you can argue for once the feeling has been disciplined into form.

But what if this gets the sequence wrong? What if the pull is not just the biographical accident that precedes real inquiry, but is itself a form of knowing — the first move of an understanding that has not yet become propositional?

The philosopher Michael Polanyi spent much of his career arguing exactly this. For Polanyi, the scientist's sense that a particular problem has depth — that this direction is worth pursuing in ways that adjacent directions are not — is not psychology in the pejorative sense. It is what he called intellectual passion: the practitioner's accumulated experience and understanding operating below the level of explicit articulation, detecting a pattern or opening that conscious reasoning has not yet caught up to. He called the developed capacity for this connoisseurship — a trained sensitivity, built through years of dwelling inside a domain, to what is significant and what is merely technically interesting. The expert feels the worth of a problem before they can say what the worth is, because the feeling is the tacit knowledge speaking.

The cognitive psychologists Anders Ericsson and Walter Kintsch arrived at a related conclusion from a different direction. Their work on expert memory showed that years of deliberate practice build what they call retrieval structures — vast, organised networks of domain knowledge that operate implicitly, faster than conscious attention can track. When an expert encounters something relevant, the retrieval structure activates, and the result is experienced as a felt sense of significance: this matters, this is interesting, something is here. The feeling is not arbitrary. It is the expert's accumulated knowledge base responding to a pattern — compressed and delivered as intuition before the reasoning catches up.

This suggests something counterintuitive about the relationship between expertise and felt pull. The researcher who has genuinely dwelt inside a domain — who has lived toward it over years, in Ingold's sense — develops an interest that is increasingly epistemically loaded. The pulls are more reliable, not less, than a novice's attractions, precisely because they are informed by a richer implicit knowledge of what has depth and what does not. Interest, on this account, is not what you have before you know things. It is what knowing things feels like from the inside, when the knowing is operating ahead of articulation.

There is something worth pausing on in the phenomenology of this. When people describe what draws them to a subject, they often use passive language. The topic found me. I kept returning to it without quite knowing why. It felt less like a choice than like a recognition. This is not merely poetic imprecision. It corresponds to something the cognitive architecture suggests is real: the retrieval structure responds to patterns; the scientist undergoes the response. The felt pull arrives before the choosing. Which raises a question about what we are actually doing when we follow an interest — whether we are making a decision or reading a signal that something has already been detected.

The creativity researcher Dean Keith Simonton has spent decades studying what distinguishes the most productive scientific careers from the merely competent ones. His finding is not that great scientists are better at selecting problems by explicit criteria. It is that their interests are more reliably oriented toward problems with genuine depth — and that they follow those interests with persistence and breadth rather than narrowly optimising for anticipated outputs. The productive scientist is not the one who makes the best arguments for their research questions in advance. It is the one whose felt pull is well-calibrated to problem-worth, and who trusts it enough to keep following it when the justification is not yet available.

This is where the institutional picture becomes uncomfortable. The peer review and funding systems that govern most research are designed to evaluate justified claims, not pre-articulable pulls. And the empirical literature suggests they do this with a specific and measurable bias. Studies of grant review consistently find that the most novel proposals — precisely those where the interest precedes the justification, where the researcher is pulled toward territory that cannot yet be fully mapped — are systematically scored lower than more incremental work within established paradigms. The reviewers are not being unreasonable. They are evaluating what can be evaluated. But what can be evaluated is not the same as what is worth pursuing. The most epistemically valuable directions may be precisely those that look least justified from within the existing framework, because the existing framework has not yet generated the concepts needed to justify them.

The philosopher L. A. Paul made a related argument about transformative experience: you cannot evaluate in advance what following a particular path will reveal, because the knowing that would let you evaluate it is exactly the knowing that following the path will produce. A funding system that requires prior justification for a research direction is asking researchers to evaluate their interest from within the pre-interest epistemic position — which is, for the most important directions, structurally impossible. The preliminary data requirement — needing funding to generate the results that would justify the funding — is the institutional expression of this paradox, and it falls most heavily on the researchers whose pulls are most genuinely novel.

None of this is an argument that every felt pull should be followed without scrutiny. The signal/noise problem is real. Some pulls are genuine connoisseurship operating ahead of articulation. Others are ego-driven compulsion, anxiety dressed up as curiosity, or the comfortable momentum of what you have always done. The difference matters, and it is not always easy to detect from the inside. What Simonton's work suggests is that the appropriate response to this problem is not to reduce one's generative engagement but to develop the discernment that can evaluate what the engagement produces — to build, alongside the capacity for felt pull, the capacity to distinguish what is genuinely opening from what is merely familiar or flattering.

This is perhaps where contemplative practice enters not as a source of alternative knowledge but as a training in a specific kind of attention: the capacity to sit with a felt pull long enough to notice what it actually is, rather than immediately converting it into either action or justification. To ask, before the machinery of proposal-writing begins: is this genuine interest, or is this something else wearing interest's clothes? The question is not a new one. But it may be one that our institutions have stopped making space for — and the cost of that, in knowledge that remains unproduced because no one could follow the pull toward it without professional consequence, is probably larger than it looks.

What draws you to the work you do? And when did you last treat that as a question worth sitting with seriously?
