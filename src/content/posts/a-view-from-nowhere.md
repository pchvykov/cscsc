What if the most honest epistemology isn't the one that explains the most, but the one that admits it doesn't know everything — and means it? Not as a polite caveat, not as "we'll fill in the gaps later," but as a live, working stance: an *open* epistemology, where the framework you stand on is itself something you're willing to let go of. That's harder than it sounds. It isn't learning a new fact inside your existing picture of the world. It's a shift in the sense of world and self, and it has to happen from nowhere in particular — no anchor, nothing held fixed while the rest moves. Try to describe it and you've already smuggled in a place to stand. This is, more or less, what contemplative traditions have spent millennia pointing at. And it's what's actually at stake in the question of whether we can take another worldview seriously at all.

Here's the move that gets us into it. Every framework for understanding the world smuggles in its own assumptions about what *counts* as understanding in the first place. The questions we ask, the standards we apply, the things our attention picks out as significant — none of these are neutral instruments. They were shaped by the very tradition of inquiry we're operating inside.

Which leads to a question that's trickier than it looks. When we run into a worldview that doesn't share our assumptions about evidence, causation, or the nature of the self — are we actually engaging with it? Or are we quietly translating it into something more familiar, and then engaging with *that*?

The translation problem is real, and it keeps coming up. You see it in the history and philosophy of science, where people working through paradigm shifts have noticed something striking: moving between fundamentally different frameworks isn't like switching between two languages that share the same grammar. It's more like the difference between translating a text and becoming a native speaker. A translator works across a gap — converting propositions from one system into equivalents in another, inevitably losing whatever doesn't map cleanly. A native speaker doesn't experience the gap at all. The framework isn't a lens they look through; it's the medium in which thought happens.

The implication is uncomfortable. Genuinely different ways of knowing may not be commensurable in the way that rational comparison requires. You can't fully evaluate a framework from outside it, because evaluation is itself a framework-dependent activity.

Now — this doesn't slide straight into relativism, the move that says all worldviews are equally valid because none can be assessed from a neutral standpoint. That conclusion is itself made from inside a particular epistemological tradition, one that assumes the relevant question is whether frameworks are valid or not. What it might lead to instead is something more interesting: genuine curiosity. An interest in what a different worldview makes visible that ours tends to occlude — and some honesty about what ours makes visible that others might miss.

Take consciousness and experience, which sits near the centre of so many current arguments about the limits of scientific materialism. Materialism has been enormously productive — its methods have generated knowledge that is genuinely cumulative, genuinely shareable, and genuinely useful across huge differences of culture and context. That's not nothing. But the framework also carries commitments about what kinds of phenomena count as real, what kinds of explanation feel satisfying, and what kinds of experience get to count as evidence.

So when it bumps into reports that don't fit — the sense of contact with something beyond the self, the felt certainty of insight in contemplative or psychedelic states, the experience of a world populated by agencies and presences — it tends to reach for one of two moves. Reduction: *this is what's really happening neurologically.* Or dismissal: *this isn't the kind of thing that generates knowledge.* Neither move is obviously wrong. But both foreclose the question rather than opening it.

The psychedelic literature is a specific case worth lingering on. Careful empirical and philosophical work suggests that certain substances can produce experiences people describe as among the most significant of their lives — experiences that carry a strong sense of revelation, of contact, of something having become clear. The philosophical question isn't whether these experiences are real, or even whether they carry information. It's about how we approach what they might be offering.

Here's the rub: the very mechanism that generates the sense of clarity and certainty in these states doesn't discriminate between insights that are, by other measures, well-grounded and those that aren't. That's not a reason to dismiss the experiences. It's a reason to ask what kind of engagement with them is honest — and what verification could even look like, when the experience itself resists the usual forms of it.

Part of what gets lost in translation is this: the traditions that developed sustained practices around these states — contemplative, shamanic, initiatory — didn't typically frame the experiences as generating propositions to be evaluated. They framed them as transformations to be undergone, inside a context of transmission, community, and accumulated wisdom about what the transformation was *for*. Move the experience out of that context and into a framework where its output gets assessed as information, and you've changed what the experience is being asked to do. The experience itself may not change. But the relationship to it does — and that relationship may be a big part of what the tradition was actually cultivating.

None of this tells us which worldview to inhabit, or whether it's possible to move between them in ways that are genuinely enriching rather than superficially syncretic. Those stay open. But taking another worldview seriously — seriously enough that it might actually change something — probably takes more than assessing it against our existing standards and noting that it partially measures up. It might mean asking what our existing standards *can't* see, and being curious enough to sit with that question before reaching for an answer we already have.

The most honest position here may not be confidence in any particular framework, but a kind of active, attentive uncertainty. Aware that the tools we use to understand shape what we can find. Genuinely interested in what lies at the edges of their reach. Willing to engage with different ways of knowing without immediately demanding they justify themselves in our language.

That's harder than it sounds. It may also be where taking another worldview seriously actually starts.
