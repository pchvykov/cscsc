There is a story we tend to tell about modern science in which the philosophy comes first. Nature, it turns out, is mechanical. Matter is the fundamental stuff of things. Consciousness is what brains do. And from these conclusions — arrived at through rigorous investigation — certain practical orientations follow: we can study nature, model it, predict it, and intervene in it for human benefit. The ethics, on this account, is downstream of the metaphysics.

But what if the sequence runs the other way?

Francis Bacon, who more than anyone else gave modern science its institutional shape and its animating purpose, was remarkably honest about this. His project was not to discover what nature is and then decide what to do with that knowledge. It began from a moral conviction: that human suffering is an affront, that natural limitation is an obstacle, and that knowledge has an obligation to address both. When Bacon imagined his ideal research institution — Salomon's House, in his utopian fable The New Atlantis — its purpose was not the pursuit of truth as a good in itself but to "effect all things possible" in the service of human welfare. The metaphysics of mechanism was not the premise of the Baconian project. It was a tool adequate to a moral purpose that had already been decided. The ethics came first.

This is not, on its own, a criticism. The moral weight of the Baconian inheritance is real and should not be minimised. A friend with diabetes who is alive because of insulin. A child with bacterial meningitis who recovers because of antibiotics. The reduction in infant mortality that has transformed human life over the last two centuries — from roughly one in three children dying before the age of five, to fewer than one in twenty globally today. Any serious engagement with the question of what science is for has to begin here, with the genuine and enormous goods that the project of mastering natural causes for the relief of suffering has produced. The steel man for modern science is not a philosophical argument. It is a person who is alive because of it.

And yet. The same ethical stance that generates those goods — the orientation toward nature as obstacle, toward constraint as enemy, toward the world as a reservoir of problems to be solved — also generates something else. It shapes what can be seen and what cannot. It determines which questions feel worth asking and which feel like category errors. A forest, approached from within this stance, shows up primarily as timber yield, carbon sequestration potential, or biodiversity metric — all of which are real and important, but none of which quite captures what a forest is when you are standing inside one. An estuary becomes a flood management system. A community of soil organisms becomes an input variable in an agricultural model. These framings are not wrong, but they are selective in ways that the stance itself makes difficult to notice.

The philosopher C. S. Lewis, writing in the middle of the twentieth century, put the problem with unusual sharpness. The conquest of nature, he observed, is never quite what it presents itself as. It is always, in practice, some people's power over other people with nature as the instrument. And what it requires, at the level of formation, is the removal of what he called the Tao — the trained moral sentiments, the capacity for affective relationship with the world, the sense that things might have a kind of dignity that places limits on what can be done to them. Think of the difference between a marine biologist who studies coral reefs as systems to be modelled and a marine biologist who experiences the reef as something that can be lost — not merely as a data point, but as something whose loss would be a genuine diminishment of the world. Lewis's argument is that the training that produces excellent scientists systematically removes the conditions under which the second orientation can be cultivated. It produces technical mastery at the cost of the affective relationship that might orient that mastery toward genuinely good ends.

The question this opens is whether a different ethical stance could produce a different science — not just a differently motivated science using the same methods, but something that would actually observe different things, ask different questions, notice what the current orientation makes invisible. The philosopher Emmanuel Levinas spent his career arguing that ethics precedes ontology: that the encounter with the other — the face that looks back, the being that exceeds your categories — is prior to and more fundamental than any metaphysical framework you bring to it. Applied to scientific practice, this is a significant reorientation. It would mean beginning not with your model of what the organism is, but with a posture of attention to what the organism is doing that you have not yet understood. Some researchers have begun calling this agential maximalism: the methodological decision to presume agency and interiority in living systems rather than requiring proof of it. The biologist Michael Levin's work on the electrical and cognitive dimensions of cell collectives is one example — by asking what a group of cells might be trying to do rather than merely what it mechanically does, he has arrived at observations that the standard null assumption of mechanism would have made invisible.

Whether this constitutes a genuine shift in scientific practice or a reframing of the same outputs is genuinely unclear, and the question deserves more careful attention than it usually receives. What the empirical psychology of gratitude does suggest — and there is a surprisingly robust literature on this — is that the difference between a receptive and an extractive orientation is not merely affective. Gratitude as a stable dispositional stance, not a momentary feeling, changes what people attend to, what they value, and how they interpret what happens to them. There is a practice used in some Indigenous scientific gatherings — the Thanksgiving Address of the Haudenosaunee, which names and acknowledges each part of the living world before any business begins — that is sometimes cited as an example of what it might look like to begin from gratitude rather than extraction. The Address is not a preamble. It is an extended, embodied act of attention to everything that sustains the people in the room before they turn to their own concerns. Whether something like that could be translated into a scientific conference or a research institution without becoming performance is an open question — but it points toward what the difference between prologue and premise might actually feel like in practice.

The harder question is whether this can be institutionalised without immediately becoming its own form of performance. Land acknowledgement that precedes business as usual is not a reorientation; it is a ritual that confirms the existing structure by cordoning off its critique at the beginning and then proceeding regardless. Gratitude becomes foundational only when it restructures the aim of the inquiry, not when it prefaces it. The difference between a prologue and a premise is everything, and building that difference into institutions — into the rhythms and rituals and physical spaces through which science is actually practised — is a problem that good intentions cannot solve on their own.

Martin Heidegger, whose diagnosis of modern technology is among the most searching available, was pessimistic about individual-level solutions. The enframing — the mode of disclosure that makes nature show up as standing-reserve, as resource ordered for human use — is not a choice that individual scientists make and could decide to unmake. It operates at the level of civilisational habit, shaping what can appear as real before any individual brings their ethical orientation to it. A researcher who privately cultivates gratitude and attentiveness, and then returns to an institution whose incentive structures reward novelty, productivity, and the compression of reality into publishable models, is swimming against a current that the individual ethical stance alone cannot reverse.

This does not make the project of ethical reorientation pointless. It makes it harder and slower than the conversation sometimes implies, and it suggests that the ethical question and the institutional question cannot be separated. What would it look like to take this seriously at the level of how scientists are formed, what rituals frame their gatherings, what spaces they work in, what the implicit curriculum of their training actually teaches? A laboratory that began each week with a structured practice of attention to the organisms it was studying — not as data sources but as beings with their own complexity — would be doing something different from one that did not, even if the methods were identical. A funding body that rewarded researchers for the quality of their attention rather than the novelty of their outputs would produce different science, over time, than one that does not.

These are not questions with obvious answers. But they may be among the more important ones a community interested in the future of knowledge could be sitting with. We would love to hear what resonates from your own experience. Does the framing of resentment and gratitude as epistemic stances ring true? Is there a moment in your own research or practice where a shift in orientation — from control to attention, from extraction to receptivity — changed what you were able to see? And what, if anything, do you think would actually have to change — in practice, not just in aspiration — for a different kind of science to become possible?
