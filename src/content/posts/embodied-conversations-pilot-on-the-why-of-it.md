We've already announced the Embodied Conversations pilot, so we just wanted to dig a little deeper into the why.

The short version: most of our collective inquiry happens from the neck up. We read, we argue, we analyze, and somewhere along the way the rest of us — the part that tenses, softens, leans forward, goes quiet — gets left in the hallway. Embodied Conversations is one (among many other out there!) experiment in inviting that part back into the room. Not as a replacement for thinking, but as its ground. The orientation is feeling first: noticing what something does in us before we decide what we think about it, and trusting that this noticing is itself a form of inquiry. Concretely, each session is a threefold inquiry — into self, into others, and into the symbol at the center — and the real practice is holding the three together: letting what we find in ourselves, in each other, and in the thing we've gathered around inform one another rather than run on separate tracks.

A few words have been organizing how we understand the form. We offer them here partly as explanation and partly as equipment — they're meant to be used.

The first, and the most important, is **chorus**. The chorus is what we're calling the space in the middle — though it's less a space than a voice. It names the unity of the vocal group: everyone present, sensed as one body, one unfolding voice that each of us is participating in rather than performing for. The word's history already carries most of this: the Greek *khoros* named both the band of dancers and singers in ritual and drama *and* the dancing-ground itself — the enclosed place where the dance happened. (Deeper down it shares a root with *garden*, *yard*, and *court*: an enclosure.) So a chorus was never just voices; it was voices, bodies, and the held space that made their movement one thing. The conversation, on this understanding, isn't a sequence of individual contributions but the movement of the chorus itself — and the practice is to participate in that unfolding of collective voice, through inquiry into self, into others, and into the symbol we've gathered around.

Why name it at all? Because once the chorus has a name, we can speak *about* it without leaving it. The name gives us a shared handle for going meta gently: "something feels missing from the chorus," "the chorus is leaning conceptual," "the texture of the chorus just changed." Without a name, those observations tend to come out as critiques of individuals or of the conversation; with one, they become observations of a body we all belong to and are all responsible for. Naming the field is what lets us tend it together.

The second is **radiance** — the sensing of the center. In a recent community call (April, one on the medieval episteme), we found ourselves circling something we didn't yet have a word for: the act of sensing the middle of a gathering, of becoming more attuned to it. Radiance is offered here as that word. It names a shift in the aim of conversation itself: the point is not to make distinctions — to carve, classify, take positions — but to gravitate, to orbit around a center. And that center is the chorus: the live, somewhat mysterious middle we are gathered around. The etymology carries the geometry. *Radiance* descends from *radius* — a ray, but originally the spoke of a wheel: the line that runs between rim and hub. The center is not mute; it radiates. To practice radiance is to register what's reaching you along your own spoke — a warmth, a pull, a change in texture — and to let your speaking and listening tune to it. Less argument, more orbit.

The third is **symbol** — the thing we use to hold it all together. Each session places something at the center of the circle; for the first session, that's Barthes' essay on wrestling. We call it a symbol rather than a topic because of what the word originally meant. The Greek *symbolon* comes from *symballein*, "to throw together": it was a physical token — a coin or knucklebone broken in two between hosts and guests, or parties to a pact — each keeping half. Recognition happened when the halves were brought back together and fit. A symbol, in other words, is a ritual device for *holding together*: the half you carry implies the half you lack, and meaning arrives in the rejoining. (Its etymological opposite is the *diabolic* — *diaballein*, to throw apart.) So the text at the center of our circle is a symbolon in the old sense: each of us arrives carrying a half of something, and the session is the throwing-together. The symbol holds the middle the way a fire holds the middle of a gathering — not because the fire is the point, but because it gives everyone somewhere to face. The chorus is the real center; radiance is how we sense it; the symbol is what holds the gathering to it.

That's the why, more or less: a hunch that there are modes of collective understanding that only become available when the body is included, when a conversation orbits its center rather than carving up its territory, and when the voice doing the inquiring is, at least sometimes, ours rather than mine.

## A Vocabulary for the First Session

Since the first symbol is *The World of Wrestling*, we've been assembling a small lexicon. Not all of it is Barthes' own vocabulary — some terms are borrowed from elsewhere, some are ours — but together they circle the essay's territory: performance, drama, roles, and the various tensions and comedic possibilities that live in all of these situations. It's worth reading the essay beforehand if you can (it's short, and it's a pleasure); if you don't get to it, come anyway. Either way, this list isn't required reading so much as something to ruminate over — you may find some of these words naming things you've felt but not quite said, and they may be useful handles during the conversation itself.

**Aesthetic resolution** — the satisfaction of everyone playing their role and the match ending in a resolving way; justice rendered legible

**Body centrality** — the body as the vehicle of the role; physique as sign (Barthes: the wrestler's body is his first speech)

**Camp** (Sontag) — love of artifice and excess; a thing being so much itself it becomes theatre

**Dramatic nobility** — the dignity available *inside* full commitment to a role, even an embarrassing one

**Doing it for the bit** — following a thread or joke (maybe too far) to its possible extreme

**Emphasis** — Barthes' borrowed theatrical term; nothing suggested, everything underlined

**Exaggerating** — making more of something than is "there"; quantitative excess

**Front stage / back stage** (Goffman) — the performed self vs. the self at rest; wrestling as front stage with no pretense of a back

**Getting carried away** — when someone takes their role/title too seriously at the cost of human connection (see also: role-reliance)

**Grandiloquence** — excess raised to a style; every gesture amplified to total legibility (Barthes' own term)

**Histrionics** — performance tipped into embarrassment; display that seems needy of being seen

**Kayfabe** — the wrestling contract of maintained pretense; treating the staged as real

**Leaning into** — embracing the role rather than holding it at ironic distance; commitment without apology

**Mugging** — knowing, winking overplay; exaggeration that owns itself (face-first)

**Obviousness of the roles** — wrestling's honesty: the signs are total, nothing is hidden in interiority

**Performing** — stepping into a role with craft; inhabiting rather than deceiving

**Performative tension** — the charge inherent in any role: performing is always a bit of a performance. Not a flaw to eliminate but a tension to work with — usable well or badly, never absent

**Pretending** — performance that aims to conceal itself; deception rather than display

**Putting on a performance** — becoming a spectacle; the artifice itself made visible, for better or worse

**Self-reliance / role-reliance** — self-reliance: a sense of self rooted deeper than any role; role-reliance: leaning too heavily on one's roles for meaning, with the self-importance that follows. The aim, roughly: self-reliance *with* full performativity of roles

**Theatrum mundi** — the old topos that all the world's a stage: we are never not in some role; the question is only how we hold it

## Questions to Feel Our Way Through

For a sense of the essay's texture before you arrive: Barthes places wrestling in the lineage of ancient theatre — the grandiloquence of Greek drama, the masks, the second-rate suburban halls standing in for amphitheatres, all of it bathed in *a light without shadow*, which for him generates an emotion without reserve. Nothing hidden, nothing held in suspense; each moment fully intelligible on its own, meaning total at every instant rather than deferred to a result.

Out of that, some of the questions we might feel our way through together — offered as starting points, not an agenda:

How is justice *performed*? In the ring, Barthes says, the match exists so that Justice can be staged and made visible — the bastard cheats precisely so that his punishment can be rendered legible. Where in our own lives is justice something performed rather than merely decided, and what do we feel when we watch it land?

Why do we love it when people fill their roles expectedly? There's a real pleasure — maybe even a *dramatic nobility* — in someone committing fully to their part, and a corresponding ease in knowing roughly what trajectories to expect from one another. What is that pleasure made of, and how does it feed our sense of *aesthetic resolution* — the satisfaction of everyone playing their role and the whole thing ending in a resolving way?

Can we allow ourselves to fully lean into something? Under what conditions do we — and when does leaning in feel dignified, and when does it tip into embarrassment? Is there a difference, in our own bodies, between performing, putting on a performance, and pretending?

And the quieter one underneath all of these: what would it mean to bring wrestling's honesty — its total legibility, its refusal to hide anything in interiority — into a conversation? What would a chorus look like with nothing held in suspense?
