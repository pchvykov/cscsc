Building on recent conversations in our community about research monasteries, this post aims to introduce three case studies that provide further examples to think through this theme. The idea that keeps surfacing is compelling and also genuinely difficult: a place, or a network of places, where serious intellectual inquiry and serious contemplative practice are held together as integrated activities — not one supplementing the other, but both as necessary dimensions of a single endeavour. Responses to the [earlier post](/blog/what-would-a-research-monastery-look-like/) touched on questions that any serious attempt would have to face: how does such a place support itself economically? How do you balance personal space and time with genuine community life? What does self-sufficiency actually look like in practice? And how do you handle the inevitable frictions that arise when people try to live and work closely together over time? Rather than approaching these questions in the abstract, it seems worth looking carefully at three institutions that have each, in their own way, tried to hold some version of them. None is a perfect model. But each illuminates a different dimension of what building something like this actually requires.

The Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton is the closest thing the twentieth century produced to a pure research community. Founded in 1930 on an explicit anti-utilitarian philosophy — the idea that genuine intellectual breakthroughs require freedom from teaching, administration, and the pressure to produce applicable work — it has housed Einstein, Gödel, and Clifford Geertz, among others. There are no students, no departments in the conventional sense, no tuition income. Visiting scholars arrive for a year or two with no formal obligations beyond their research. The campus is physically designed around a specific rhythm: individual offices for solitude, and a communal afternoon tea that functions as the mechanism through which interdisciplinary encounter actually happens. Not by schedule, not by mandate — by proximity and a shared table.

What makes IAS work is also what makes it difficult to replicate: a founding endowment now worth roughly 1.6 billion dollars, which severs the link between research output and income completely. The design principle — that genuine intellectual freedom requires insulation from grant cycles and immediate output pressure — is probably correct, and resonates with what several community members noted: that depending on someone else's money means depending on their opinion. The mechanism for achieving that insulation, though, is a capitalisation event that most institutions will never have. IAS also transmits something beyond its official research output: working habits, intellectual style, modes of attention passed between permanent faculty and visiting scholars through proximity and informal conversation. But this happens incidentally, not by design — which means it is fragile, and dependent on individual faculty who happen to value it.

Black Mountain College is a stranger and in some ways more instructive case. Founded in 1933 in rural North Carolina, it had no fixed curriculum, no departments, no grades in its early years, no tenure, and no external board. Faculty owned and ran the institution collectively, ate communally, farmed together, and lived on campus. The arts were central — Josef Albers ran the design programme; John Cage, Merce Cunningham, Buckminster Fuller, and Robert Rauschenberg all taught or studied there. It lasted twenty-four years, a remarkable run for an institution operating almost entirely on ideological subsidy — faculty accepting below-market salaries, sometimes going without pay for months, because the model was worth it to them personally.

What Black Mountain illuminates most clearly is the possibility of intentional tacit knowledge transmission. Albers' foundation course in design — structured exercises working with paper, wire, cardboard, understanding material properties through manipulation before representation — was a pedagogy of the embodied. Students who went through it could not easily describe what they had learned. They could only show it in subsequent work. This is perhaps the most generative lesson Black Mountain offers: tacit knowledge can be intentionally structured through practice, not just absorbed through proximity. The question it opens for any research monastery is whether something analogous can be designed — structured practices of inquiry and attention that develop modes of knowing that cannot be captured in papers.

Black Mountain also surfaced a question that any genuinely non-hierarchical knowledge community will eventually have to face: what happens when serious disagreement arises and there is no agreed mechanism for resolving it? The college experienced several significant schisms over its lifetime. This connects to something raised in the community discussion — the observation that the most durable intentional communities tend to be monasteries, partly because they have developed explicit structures for managing the kinds of conflict that quietly exhaust less structured communities. The relationship between participation, autonomy, and institutional continuity is one of the harder design questions any such community faces, and Black Mountain encountered it honestly.

The Monastic Academy — MAPLE — in Vermont is the most recent experiment and the most explicitly contemplative. Founded in 2012, it operates as a residential community combining several hours of daily meditation with collective physical work, communal meals, and intellectual inquiry focused on long-term thinking and civilizational resilience. The economic model is a mix of program fees, donations, and the labour of long-term residents who receive room, board, and practice training in exchange for their participation. This is the monastic donation economy, updated — and it works, within its own terms. One question it raises is about who can participate: the model is most accessible to the young and unencumbered, which makes it harder to draw in established intellectual practitioners with developed research programmes, the kind of people who might make the research monastery vision most fully coherent.

What MAPLE has developed with particular depth is its contemplative curriculum. The core practice is somatic rather than conceptual — transmitting capacities of attention and sustained presence that are only partially accessible through verbal instruction. The transmission mechanism is practice itself, structured time, and a community of people who have gone further along the path. What remains more open is how this contemplative depth connects to rigorous intellectual inquiry. MAPLE has a clear intellectual orientation but its research programme is still developing. In this sense it is interestingly complementary to IAS — which has the research infrastructure but has never systematically cultivated the contemplative or embodied practices that might deepen it.

There is also a fourth reference point worth naming here, even if it is fictional: Neal Stephenson's novel Anathem, which several community members mentioned, imagines scholarly communities — concents — that sustain themselves through agriculture, limited trade with the outside world, and periodic public exchange during festivals where scholars open their doors and share their work. It is a thought experiment rather than a blueprint, but it captures something real about the appeal of the idea: the combination of self-sufficiency, genuine intellectual depth, and a rhythmic relationship with the wider world that neither walls itself off completely nor dissolves into it.

What do these cases reveal, placed alongside each other? On economics, they map a spectrum from maximum capital insulation to maximum ideological subsidy to a hybrid donation economy — and none is straightforwardly replicable. What they share is a common requirement: genuine independence from immediate output pressure needs either accumulated capital, participant willingness to absorb economic cost, or a community willing to support the institution's work without attaching conditions to it. As one community member put it, the question of modes of production — what is actually being made at such a place, and how that sustains the people making it — may be the most practically urgent question any such project faces.

On the balance between solitude and community, no institution has clearly achieved both simultaneously. IAS is excellent at solitude and patchier on genuine communal intellectual life. Black Mountain and MAPLE are structured around community, which produces extraordinary creative conditions and also requires careful attention to individual renewal. Several voices in the community discussion were clear on this: personal space and time are not luxuries but necessities for both contemplative and research practice, and any structure that makes genuine solitude feel like a transgression against community is unlikely to sustain the kind of people it most needs. A research monastery would need to design for both — not as a compromise, but as two distinct things each given its proper architecture.

And on tacit transmission, the most consistent finding across all three cases is that it requires intentional design rather than proximity alone. The institution would need to have a sense of what it is trying to transmit before it can design the transmission. And working out what that actually is — what kinds of knowing matter, and how they develop — might itself be the first and most important inquiry such an institution could undertake.
